# Bradley-Terry

## TL;DR — when to pick it

Enable Bradley-Terry when you want the **statistically principled
counterpart to ELO** — the underlying model ELO is approximating —
and you have enough matches (tens per item or more) that maximum
likelihood is meaningful. Use it as a sanity check on the live ELO
ranking, for offline analysis where you can afford a couple hundred
milliseconds, or in any context where "we fit a Bradley-Terry model"
is a sentence you need to write in a paper.

## Why ELO is a shortcut

Bradley-Terry came first. In 1952, Ralph Bradley and Milton Terry
wrote a paper ("Rank analysis of incomplete block designs: I. The
method of paired comparisons") proposing a simple probabilistic
model: give each item a positive latent strength $\pi_i$, and say
that the probability $i$ beats $j$ in a single match is

$$ P(i \text{ beats } j) = \frac{\pi_i}{\pi_i + \pi_j} $$

That's the whole model. If you see a pile of match results, you can
**fit** it by finding the $\pi_i$ values that maximize the
likelihood of the data. That gives you a "best explanation" ranking.

ELO, which came a decade later, is a *sequential* approximation to
Bradley-Terry. Each match, ELO does one gradient step on the
log-likelihood, in a specific parameterization where $R_i = 400
\log_{10} \pi_i$. The 400 is cosmetic; the connection is exact.

So why does `elo_engine` ship both? Because they answer different
questions:

- **ELO** answers "what's my best guess *right now*, after this
  match I just saw"? Online, cheap, slightly noisy.
- **Bradley-Terry** answers "given my entire history, what's the
  joint maximum likelihood estimate"? Batch, slower, more stable on
  large datasets.

When the data is thin, they agree. When the data is thick,
Bradley-Terry is slightly better — it's integrating over the whole
history instead of one step at a time.

## The MLE, intuitively

Here's what "maximum likelihood estimation" means for Bradley-Terry.

Imagine you have three items A, B, C and you observed:
- A beat B, 4 times
- B beat A, 1 time
- A beat C, 3 times
- C beat A, 2 times
- B beat C, 2 times
- C beat B, 1 time

You want to pick strengths $\pi_A, \pi_B, \pi_C$ so that the
**predicted** outcomes match the **observed** outcomes as closely as
possible. "As closely as possible" means: maximize the probability
that this exact data would have been generated by those strengths.

The log-likelihood is
$$ \mathcal{L}(\pi) = \sum_{i,j} w_{ij} \log \frac{\pi_i}{\pi_i + \pi_j} $$
where $w_{ij}$ is the number of times $i$ beat $j$.

It turns out this has a **nice iterative solution** called the
Minorization-Maximization (MM) algorithm (Hunter, 2004):
$$ \pi_i^{\text{new}} = \frac{W_i}{\sum_{j \neq i} \frac{N_{ij}}{\pi_i + \pi_j}} $$

where $W_i$ is total wins by $i$ and $N_{ij}$ is total matches
between $i$ and $j$. Start with $\pi_i = 1$ for everyone, apply
the update, repeat until the $\pi$s stop moving (usually a few
dozen iterations). Normalize so $\sum \pi_i = 1$. Sort by $\pi$.
You have Bradley-Terry's ranking.

## Worked example

Two items: A and B. A beat B 3 times, B beat A 1 time.

Start: $\pi_A = \pi_B = 0.5$.

Update $\pi_A$:
- $W_A = 3$.
- Denominator: $N_{AB} / (\pi_A + \pi_B) = 4 / 1.0 = 4$.
- $\pi_A^{\text{new}} = 3/4 = 0.75$.

Update $\pi_B$:
- $W_B = 1$.
- Denominator: $4 / (0.75 + 0.5) = 3.2$.
- $\pi_B^{\text{new}} = 1/3.2 = 0.3125$.

Normalize: $\pi_A = 0.706, \pi_B = 0.294$. The predicted probability
that A beats B is $0.706/(0.706 + 0.294) = 0.706$ — which matches
the observed win rate 3/4 = 0.75 up to regularization. Another few
iterations tighten this further. Eventually $\pi_A / (\pi_A +
\pi_B) \to 0.75$ exactly.

This demonstrates the core property: **Bradley-Terry perfectly
reproduces observed win rates when the data is consistent**. It's
only when you have contradictions (A beats B, B beats C, C beats A)
that the MLE has to pick a compromise.

## What Bradley-Terry reveals that ELO hides

Consider two items each with 10 wins and 10 losses:
- Item X: 10-10 against one mediocre opponent.
- Item Y: 10-10 against a mix of strong and weak opponents.

ELO will converge X and Y to similar ratings given enough time, but
the path there is bumpy and depends on match order. Bradley-Terry's
MLE *handles this cleanly* — it fits all data simultaneously and
recognizes that Y's mixed record against varied opposition is more
informative than X's.

This is why Bradley-Terry is the preferred tool in sports analytics
papers. It's the "correct" way to infer strengths from paired
comparisons, subject to the Bradley-Terry assumption that a single
hidden strength exists.

## Connection to logistic regression

Taking logs of the Bradley-Terry equation:
$$ \log \frac{P(i \text{ beats } j)}{P(j \text{ beats } i)} = \log \pi_i - \log \pi_j $$

The log-odds of winning is a linear function of the log-strength
difference. That's exactly the setup of **logistic regression**,
with each match's outcome as the dependent variable and a binary
vector of which items are competing as the feature. This equivalence
is why Bradley-Terry shows up in every applied statistics textbook
that covers paired comparisons.

## Failure modes

**The graph must be connected.** If items A and B have never played
each other or any common opponent (even transitively), their
strengths are not jointly identifiable. MLE-via-MM will either not
converge or settle on an arbitrary relative scale. `elo_engine`'s
implementation handles this by keeping the iteration count bounded
and by normalizing — but the resulting ranking between disconnected
clusters is meaningless.

**Perfectly dominant items.** If A has never lost, the MLE wants to
push $\pi_A \to \infty$. Hunter's MM iteration regularizes this by
finite step sizes, but the result is "very high rating" which in
practice rounds to "rank 1 by a huge margin". Which is fine.

**Sparse data.** With only 1-2 matches per item, the MLE is
extremely uncertain. Bradley-Terry does not tell you this; it just
hands you a point estimate. For sparsity-aware ranking, use
Glicko-2 or TrueSkill, which explicitly track uncertainty.

## Relationship to the other ensemble members

- **ELO** is Bradley-Terry fit by online gradient ascent with K as
  the step size. Same answer at infinity, different transients.
- **Thurstone** replaces the logistic with a Gaussian probit link.
  Almost the same model, slightly different tail behavior.
- **PageRank** and **Markov** treat the win matrix as a random walk
  graph instead of a likelihood; they don't assume a latent scalar
  strength.
- **Copeland** and **Borda** ignore the strength concept entirely
  and just count things.

When ELO and Bradley-Terry disagree on a specific item's rank, it
usually means ELO hasn't converged yet and Bradley-Terry's full-data
estimate is closer to the truth.

## In `elo_engine`

- Computed inside `compareAlgorithms()` when
  `AlgorithmId.bradleyTerry` is enabled.
- Exposed as `RankingComparison.bradleyTerryRanking` —  sorted
  descending by the MLE latent strengths.
- The iteration runs to a fixed count (no early exit); cost is
  $O(N^2 \cdot I)$ where $I$ is the iteration budget (~50 in
  `elo_engine`'s implementation).

## One-sentence summary

Bradley-Terry is ELO done right when you can afford to batch — full
MLE over the whole history instead of ELO's online single gradient
step per match.
